What am I looking at?

The spacetime

You sit on a point mass $M$ in 2+1 dimensional de Sitter space. As you radiate mass outward, the geometry is the outgoing Vaidya–de Sitter solution, which is exact:

$$\begin{gathered} ds^2 = -f\,du^2 - 2\,du\,dr + r^2 d\phi^2,\\ f(u,r) = 1 - 8GM(u) - \frac{r^2}{\ell^2}. \end{gathered}$$

Here $u$ is retarded time and $r$ is the areal radius (circumference$/2\pi$). In three dimensions a point mass makes no black hole. It makes a cone with deficit angle $2\pi\bigl(1-\sqrt{1-8GM}\bigr)$, and your cosmological horizon sits at

$$r_c = \ell\sqrt{1-8GM},\qquad \frac{S}{S_{\mathrm{dS}}} = \frac{r_c}{\ell}.$$

Units

Lengths and times are in units of $\ell$, with $c=1$. In 2+1 dimensions $G$ has units of 1/mass, so masses are given in units of $1/8G$. The maximum mass is $M = 1/8G$: the deficit reaches $2\pi$ and the horizon shrinks to zero. $M=0$ is empty de Sitter, with $r_c=\ell$.

The sky

Every object is drawn where it was when it emitted the light reaching you now, i.e. on your past light cone, at its areal radius.

Colour and redshift

Between shells, light moves along $v = u + 2r_*(r)=\text{const}$ with $dr_*/dr = 1/f$. Ray tracing through every shell the light crossed gives the exact redshift

$$\begin{gathered} 1+z = \frac{d\tau_{\text{you}}}{d\tau_{\text{src}}} = \frac{\sqrt{1-8GM_{\text{now}}}}{\dot u\, f\, D},\\ D=\frac{dv}{du_{\text{arr}}}, \end{gathered}$$

where $\dot u$ is the source's $du/d\tau$ at emission, and $D$ picks up a factor $f_{\text{new}}/f_{\text{old}}$ at each shell crossing. At launch the beacons' light reaches you stretched by $\sqrt{(1+v)/(1-v)} \approx 45$, so they transmit in the far UV, tuned so you first receive them at 450 nm. The numbers count messages, sent at a rate that reaches you as one per $0.15\,\ell$ at launch.

The three circles

  • $r = \ell$, outer dotted circle (empty dS). The horizon of empty de Sitter space, and the largest your horizon can ever get: you reach it once you have radiated all your mass.
  • $r_c$, dashed circle (horizon). The cosmological horizon set by your mass now, $r_c = \ell\sqrt{1-8GM}$. It jumps outward the moment you emit. If you emit nothing more, this is where your event horizon ends up. When the visible edge has caught up with it, the two coincide and only one circle is drawn.
  • Solid circle (visible edge). The farthest areal radius that light reaching your eye right now started from: the outermost point of your past light cone. Nothing you see can lie outside it. After an emission at time $\tau_e$ it catches up with $r_c$ as light from the newly enclosed region arrives: $$\begin{gathered} r_{\text{edge}} = \max\!\Bigl(r_c^{\text{old}},\; r_c^{\text{new}}\tanh\tfrac{\tau-\tau_e}{2\ell}\Bigr). \end{gathered}$$

The faint dotted rings inside are your earlier horizons, $r_c$ at each of your earlier masses.

The Penrose diagram

The diagram uses null coordinates $U = e^{\tau_{\text{out}}/\ell}$ and $V = \mp e^{-\tau_{\text{in}}/\ell}$, where $\tau_{\text{out}}$ ($\tau_{\text{in}}$) is your clock reading when the outgoing (ingoing) light ray through a point leaves (reaches) you, compactified with $\arctan$. Light always moves at 45°. Your worldline is $UV=-1$, $\mathscr{I}^+$ is $UV=1$, and the event horizon is $V=0$.

Solid worldlines are already determined: they lie inside your current future light cone's past, $u \le u_{\text{now}}$. Dashed continuations are predictions that assume you emit nothing more. A later shell will bend them.

Now (the default) applies $U\to U e^{-\tau/\ell},\ V\to V e^{\tau/\ell}$, a translation in static time that leaves your worldline and $\mathscr{I}^+$ fixed. The present sits mid-left, and the earlier history is squeezed against the past horizon. Once you have radiated all your mass, the neighbourhood of now is visibly the static patch of empty de Sitter.

Launching

Everything you launch leaves at $v = 0.999c$ and carries its share of your mass $\Delta M$. At that speed the mass travels with the object as a thin null shell (the exact Vaidya solution, up to $1-v = 0.1\%$), while the object can still send light back to you. This is exact for a Shell launch; a single beacon's mass is spread over a ring (s-wave approximation). Brightness is schematic.

The stars came from you. You started heavier, with mass $M_0 + M_\star$. Before $\tau = 0$ you launched the stars in six bursts, at $0.999c$ like your beacons, each burst carrying $M_\star/6$ of your mass. Like beacons, they transmit very blue light, tuned so you would have seen them in the visible at launch. That is why the game opens with a ring of older, smaller horizons and a visible edge still catching up with $r_c$.

Things to notice

  • Stars recede and redden as $1+z\propto e^{\tau/\ell}$, whatever the mass. The temperature you measure is $1/2\pi\ell$ for any $M$; the mass changes the horizon's size and entropy, not how fast things fade.
  • Images pile up at the horizon and never cross it. Each beacon has a last message you will ever receive.
  • Each frozen image sits where that object crossed your event horizon, so it records how big your horizon was then. A beacon launched after you have shed mass freezes farther out than an older one, even though the older one is now much farther away: you are still receiving its very old light.
  • When you launch, sources farther out than the launched mass turn bluer by $\sqrt{1-8GM_{\text{old}}}\big/\sqrt{1-8GM_{\text{new}}}$. Crossing the moving shell acts like a radial boost: the two cones, with different deficit angles, are glued along the expanding circle. Unlike 3+1, this step does not fade as the shell grows, because 2+1 gravity has no length scale. Each source returns to normal once the shell has passed it. The same boost kicks slower objects inward, but nothing launched at $0.999c$ is slow enough to be turned around.
